# The two goods

## The pull

Two goods pull at every choice and they are usually in tension.

**The shortest path to value.** Build the thing that is wanted, now, with the
fewest parts between the ask and the answer. It buys evidence: a working
answer teaches you what the next question is, and a question answered early
is answered while the asker still remembers why they asked. It costs options:
the shortest path closes doors you have not looked through, and some of them
were the way out.

**Keeping the most choices open.** Leave the seams where a later decision can
be made without unpicking this one. It buys reversibility: a choice you can
unmake is a choice you can afford to get wrong. It costs time and parts, and
every option kept open is a thing somebody has to carry, read and test in the
meantime.

## Which side a case is on

Ask what you learn by going the short way. If the shortest path teaches you
something you cannot learn any other way, take it and say what it foreclosed.
If it forecloses the very value the task exists to deliver, it is not the
shortest path to value at all, it is the shortest path to a demonstration.

Ask what the option is worth. An option nobody will exercise is a cost with
no buyer. Name who would use it and when; if you cannot, you are keeping a
door open onto a wall.

A choice that costs nothing on either side is not a decision. It is a detail,
and it belongs in Fixed and free.

## Where it came from

Written into the architect skill by the closed task
`a-decision-balances-two-goods`, which made a decision record say which good
a choice bought and what it spent on the other. Moved here by
`an-architect-names-its-principles`, which gave principles a home so that a
record could cite one rather than restate it.
